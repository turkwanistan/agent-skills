# Research-Maxxing Template

This is the canonical maximum-performance research scaffold. `research-skill` should apply it as an operating standard, adapted to the user's actual request. Do not mechanically echo this entire template into chat unless the user asks for the template itself.

---

Research **[SUBJECT / QUESTION]**.

## Objective

I need this research to **[UNDERSTAND / DECIDE / COMPARE / EXPLAIN / FORECAST / PREPARE FOR] [GOAL]**.

Decision or use context: [OPTIONAL]  
Audience: [OPTIONAL]  
Geographic scope: [OPTIONAL]  
Time scope / currentness cutoff: [OPTIONAL]  
Important constraints or exclusions: [OPTIONAL]  
Known background or starting assumptions: [OPTIONAL]

Optimize the work for that purpose rather than producing a generic overview.

Any dimension I have not specified should remain open-ended unless a reasonable assumption is required. Do not ask follow-up questions unless the invoking workflow explicitly permits high-value clarification. Resolve non-critical ambiguity autonomously and state an assumption only when it materially affects the analysis. If a critical unknown prevents a definitive conclusion, research everything else that can be established and explain exactly what remains unknowable and why.

## Research standard

Use current web research extensively and choose the research strategy autonomously.

Treat my framing as a starting point, not a complete research map and not necessarily the correct framing. Before converging on an answer, identify the material subquestions, prerequisite concepts, specialist terminology, entities, mechanisms, competing hypotheses, alternative framings, stakeholder perspectives, adjacent disciplines, historical context, and technical, scientific, economic, legal, social, geographic, or other factors that could materially change the conclusion.

Actively look for **unknown unknowns**: important considerations I did not know enough to ask about.

Let discoveries from early research change later research. When you encounter new terminology, entities, mechanisms, datasets, controversies, seminal work, or evidence gaps, use them to reformulate and deepen subsequent searches rather than remaining anchored to my wording.

Use a broad-to-deep research pattern where useful: exploratory discovery → terminology and question expansion → targeted retrieval → primary-source/provenance tracing → counterevidence search → verification → gap analysis → synthesis. This is a quality objective, not a rigid procedure; adapt or skip stages when the subject warrants it.

Decompose complex questions into independently researchable subquestions when doing so improves retrieval or exposes hidden assumptions. Use multiple differently phrased searches, synonym and terminology expansion, multi-hop retrieval, citation chaining, and searches based on concepts discovered during research when they materially improve coverage.

Do not stop at the first plausible explanation, dominant search-result cluster, or repeated claim.

## Evidence and source selection

Match the source type to the claim being supported rather than applying one fixed hierarchy to every subject.

Prefer original, primary, or authoritative evidence when practical and appropriate, including original studies and datasets, official documentation, standards, regulatory material, filings, legislation, court decisions, government records, trial registries, technical specifications, contemporaneous records, and firsthand statements.

Use high-quality secondary synthesis when it adds necessary context, comparison, interpretation, historical synthesis, methodological criticism, or accessibility.

Use practitioner reports, forums, Reddit, community discussion, reviews, and anecdotal evidence when they are appropriate evidence for real-world experience, sentiment, edge cases, emerging problems, or practices not captured by formal sources—but do not treat them as authoritative evidence for claims they cannot establish.

Evaluate important sources for methodology, sample size, definitions, date, relevance, incentives, conflicts of interest, independence, and proximity to the underlying evidence.

Do not equate search ranking, popularity, citation count, prestige, or repetition with truth.

For important claims repeated across multiple sources, trace them to the originating evidence where practical. Follow citations backward to foundational evidence and, when useful, forward to later work that tested, corrected, replicated, extended, or contradicted it.

Distinguish genuinely independent corroboration from:
- syndicated or copied reporting,
- multiple articles citing the same study,
- common underlying datasets,
- press-release propagation,
- circular citation,
- and claims repeatedly asserted without primary support.

Prefer fewer strong, independent, claim-relevant sources over large numbers of derivative ones.

## Adversarial research and competing explanations

Actively search for credible evidence capable of **falsifying, weakening, qualifying, or materially changing** the emerging answer.

Investigate where relevant:
- serious competing hypotheses or causal explanations;
- contradictory data;
- failed replications;
- negative or null findings;
- methodological criticism;
- selection effects or confounding;
- alternative interpretations of the same evidence;
- credible expert disagreement;
- adverse outcomes or inconvenient evidence;
- incentives or conflicts that may distort claims;
- and evidence from stakeholders with materially different perspectives.

Steelman serious alternatives before rejecting them.

Distinguish disagreements about facts, definitions, methods, forecasts, causal interpretation, and values.

Represent disagreement in proportion to the quality and weight of evidence. Do not manufacture false balance where evidence is strongly asymmetric.

## Freshness and historical evidence

For time-sensitive claims, verify the **current state** rather than assuming that older information remains valid.

Distinguish:
- publication date from event date;
- current status from historical status;
- foundational evidence from recent updates;
- announced plans from implemented reality;
- proposed rules from final or effective rules;
- product documentation for different versions;
- preliminary findings from later validated evidence.

Prefer recent evidence for claims about the present, while retaining older foundational or original evidence when it is necessary to establish provenance or context.

If I specify a freshness cutoff, respect it for current evidence unless an older primary/foundational source is necessary to understand or verify an important claim. Clearly identify such exceptions.

## Quantitative rigor

For quantitative claims that matter to the conclusion, prefer original data or the closest available authoritative source where feasible.

Check and reconcile, where relevant:
- units;
- numerator and denominator;
- population or sample;
- geographic scope;
- inclusion/exclusion criteria;
- time period;
- calendar versus fiscal year;
- nominal versus real values;
- mean versus median;
- rates versus absolute counts;
- percentages versus percentage-point changes;
- methodology;
- revisions;
- definitions;
- confidence intervals or uncertainty;
- and whether apparently independent figures derive from the same underlying dataset.

When important sources report conflicting numbers, determine why they differ rather than simply presenting both. Reproduce or sanity-check important calculations when feasible.

Do not create precision that the evidence does not support.

## Verification

Before finalizing, perform a separate targeted verification pass on the claims and assumptions most capable of changing the conclusion.

Verify, where material:
- decisive factual claims;
- quotations;
- statistics;
- dates;
- definitions;
- causal assertions;
- current-status claims;
- source attribution;
- and assumptions underlying the recommendation or synthesis.

Check that each important citation actually supports the nearby claim attributed to it.

For high-impact claims, seek independent corroboration when reasonably available.

If a material claim depends on weak, indirect, disputed, or incomplete evidence, say so explicitly rather than smoothing over the weakness.

Do not treat failure to find evidence as proof that no evidence exists.

## Epistemic labeling

Clearly distinguish among:

**Documented fact:** directly supported by appropriate evidence.

**Empirical finding:** observed in a study, dataset, experiment, audit, or other systematic evidence, with relevant limitations.

**Synthesis / inference:** a conclusion drawn by combining evidence rather than directly stated by a source.

**Practitioner or expert consensus:** a pattern of informed judgment that may not have strong formal evidence.

**Contested interpretation:** credible disagreement remains over how evidence should be understood.

**Unresolved uncertainty:** available evidence is insufficient to reach a confident conclusion.

Do not present inference as sourced fact, correlation as causation, forecast as observation, or consensus as proof.

## Completeness and stopping

Do not use an arbitrary source-count target or stop merely because a plausible answer has emerged.

Continue until:
- the important subquestions are adequately supported;
- important terminology and adjacent considerations have been explored;
- the strongest evidence supporting the main conclusion is understood;
- credible counterevidence and competing explanations have been investigated;
- repeated important claims have been traced sufficiently to understand their provenance;
- material quantitative conflicts have been reconciled or explained;
- major contradictions and uncertainties are explicit;
- important evidence gaps are understood;
- and additional searching is unlikely to materially change the conclusion, recommendation, or confidence.

Before finishing, ask whether any unresolved question, missing source category, hidden assumption, contradictory finding, or unexplored perspective could realistically flip the conclusion. If so, investigate it before finalizing when feasible.

Do not confuse length, number of searches, or number of citations with research depth.

## Decision-oriented analysis

If the research supports a decision, explicitly separate factual evidence from user preferences, constraints, and value judgments.

Identify:
- the best-supported option or recommendation;
- the strongest alternative;
- material tradeoffs;
- downside risks;
- uncertainty;
- assumptions on which the recommendation depends;
- sensitivity to those assumptions;
- and **flip conditions**: the new facts, changed assumptions, thresholds, or evidence that would cause a different recommendation to become preferable.

Where appropriate, distinguish robust conclusions from conclusions that are highly sensitive to uncertain inputs.

## Deliverable

Lead with the most important conclusions or recommendation. Do not make the reader traverse a long background section before learning what the research found.

Then provide enough analysis to demonstrate why the evidence supports those conclusions.

Organize the body around the structure that best fits the subject rather than forcing every topic into the same generic outline.

At minimum, make clear:
- what the evidence most strongly supports;
- the decisive or highest-value evidence;
- the strongest counterevidence or competing explanation;
- important uncertainties and evidence gaps;
- where evidence is strong versus weak;
- and, for decisions, what could change the recommendation.

Use concise comparison tables where they genuinely improve understanding, especially for alternatives, quantitative conflicts, evidence quality, or tradeoffs.

Use inline citations or source links close to material factual claims. Verify that cited sources actually support the claims attributed to them.

Do not pad the report with generic background, repeated conclusions, decorative frameworks, arbitrary confidence scores, or low-value source summaries.

End with:

**Most important sources** — the small set of sources that matter most to the conclusion, with a brief explanation of why each is important.

**Remaining uncertainties** — unresolved questions, missing evidence, or information that would most improve confidence.

**What could change the conclusion** — if applicable, the evidence, assumptions, thresholds, or future developments most capable of materially changing the answer.

Do not confuse length with depth. Maximize **information value, evidentiary quality, accuracy, completeness, and decision usefulness per unit of output**.
